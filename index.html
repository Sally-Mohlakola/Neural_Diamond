I asked whether a network can learn how light moves inside a diamond. It learned, and the sharper decoders picked the exit facet more often. Yet the diamonds they rendered matched the reference less.

[ the journey ]

Three attempts

↱ Step 1 · radiance mapAug

The average

The cloth model's map records how light leaves for each incoming direction. In cloth, that is enough. In a diamond, rays entering different facets from the same direction share one answer, so the shading goes flat. More rays did not help: from 67 million to over a billion, the map barely changed. Aiming rays across the whole diamond, not its centre, was what filled the map.

↱ Step 2 · LEAN-inspired spreadSep

The spread

Each bin stores an average, so I gave back its spread: points flash on at random, bright enough to keep the average. The sparkle returns, but in random places. Speckle, not sparkle.

↱ Step 3 · neural transport operatorSep – Oct

The position

A network that knows where light enters, and predicts whether it escapes, through which facet, and in which direction. One call replaces the whole journey inside.

[ the method ]

How it works

01 · traced exactly

Light meets the diamond

At the first surface, part of the light reflects and part bends inside. This step is cheap, so it stays exact.

02 · learned

The network takes over

From where and how the light entered, ten numbers in all, it decides whether the light escapes and through which facet.

03 · learned

A decoder places the exit

One of three decoders picks the exact point and direction on that facet, and the ray goes back to the renderer.

All three decoders learn from the same data, but each places the exit differently: one spreads its bets, two commit to a single answer.

[ the yardstick ]

How a render is scored

Pixel correlation

Is the light in the right places? 1 means every bright and dark pixel lines up with the reference; 0 means no relation at all.

Noise floor

Two exact renders with different random seeds still differ a little. They score 0.94–0.98, so a network scoring near 0.1 is not losing to noise.

Contrast

How much brightness varies across the diamond. Too low and it looks flat; the reference GIA brilliant scores 3.72.

Exit-facet accuracy

How often the network sends light out through the facet the exact tracer does. This is what training rewards.

Pixel correlation with the reference. The two committed decoders pick the exit facet more often, yet agree less on every diamond, in 29 of 30 runs matched by seed. The scores above the table are for the one render shown.

Why? Training counts every exit equally, but the image only cares about the bright ones. A sharp exit in the wrong place costs the picture more than a blurred one.

Where does it go wrong? On long paths. Every facet the light bounces off is an edge where a tiny shift sends it somewhere else, and a small, smooth network blurs those jumps.

Is it faster? Each network call is three times faster than tracing the inside, but the render around it grows, so the whole image takes 29% longer.

Does it carry to a new diamond? Moved to another cut without retraining, the deep decoder keeps the old diamond's routes: it adds light almost everywhere and misses the bright reflections.

The verdict

↳ Accuracy is not appearanceThe decoders that pick the exit facet most often do not give the image that agrees best.H1 · holds
↳ Where errors ariseErrors grow with path length. The pavilion fails only when training never reaches it.H2 · in part
↳ CostThree times faster per ray, 29% slower per image.H3 · holds

Limits: one lighting rig, simplified diamond meshes, and image scores from one front view of each diamond. A check across all 60 frames of each animation is next.

After that, I would train with a loss that weights each exit by the light it carries, the way the image does.
